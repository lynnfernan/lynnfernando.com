# Exit Readiness for Founders: An Operational Checklist

**For:** Founders and operator-CEOs who want options
**Reading time:** 13 minutes

---

You do not need a live process to care about exit readiness.

Readiness is operational. It is whether the company can be understood, run, and transferred without you as the narrator. That matters if you sell, recapitalize, succeed yourself, or simply want a business that does not collapse when you take a month away.

Buyers, successors, and serious operators look for the same thing: a revenue engine they can diligence, an organization that is not a single point of failure, and records that match the story. Hustle does not transfer. Systems do.

This checklist is about that transfer. It is not a valuation model, not investment advice, and not a solicitation. No one can honestly promise you a process, a buyer, or an outcome. You can make the company easier to run and easier to examine.

---

## What "Exit Ready" Means Here

Exit ready means a competent outsider could answer the commercial questions without a week of translation from the founder.

They could see how revenue is made, how it stays, where it concentrates, who owns the work, and what breaks if a key person leaves. They could trust the numbers enough to have a serious conversation.

It does **not** mean the company is for sale. It means you have options, because the business is not trapped inside your head.

| Ready enough | Not ready |
|--------------|-----------|
| The motion fits on one page and matches the CRM | The motion is a story you tell in the room |
| Forecast and finance reconcile without a late-night rebuild | Three versions of the number, all defensible in isolation |
| Customers, contracts, and pricing can be sampled | Terms live in inboxes and side letters |
| Roles and coverage are written down | The org chart is a wish list |
| You are not the only closer, the only relationship, or the only decision | A two-week vacation would stall the quarter |

If you are also trying to scale, read this next to [What Actually Moves Revenue at Growth Stage](/resources/growth-stage-revenue-infrastructure). The same infrastructure that makes a company transferable is the infrastructure that makes it scalable.

## The Five Readiness Lanes

Work the lanes in order. Do not start with a data room template if you cannot explain the engine.

### Lane 1: Revenue engine documentation

Write how money is made as if you will not be on the call.

Include the primary motion, segments, stage definitions, conversion history, cycle time, and what a qualified opportunity is. Include what you do **not** sell, and why. Outsiders waste months rediscovering your "no."

Then attach evidence: a forecast that can be sampled, win/loss notes that are honest, and a pipeline that is not a graveyard.

**Checklist**

- [ ] One-page commercial motion, current as of this quarter
- [ ] Stage definitions with exit criteria, used in the weekly review
- [ ] Conversion and cycle time by segment (not only in total)
- [ ] Win/loss reasons you would let a stranger read
- [ ] Pricing architecture and discount policy in a document, not a thread
- [ ] A sample of recent contracts that matches what you say you sell

### Lane 2: Transferable organization

Key-person risk is the quiet killer of optionality. If the founder is the brand, the closer, the product brain, and the escalation path, you have a practice, not a transferable company.

Transferable does not mean you are expendable tomorrow. It means coverage exists, decisions have owners, and a successor would know who to call.

**Checklist**

- [ ] Org chart that matches how work actually happens
- [ ] Named backups for customer, commercial, and delivery relationships
- [ ] Decision rights: who can price, hire, promise, and escalate
- [ ] A manager layer that can run the weekly commercial meeting
- [ ] Compensation and role scorecards that a new leader could inherit
- [ ] Founder time audit: where you are still the bottleneck on purpose vs. by habit

Be honest about the founder bottleneck. "I still do the important deals" is a finding, not a personality trait.

### Lane 3: Customer and contract quality

Revenue quality shows up in contracts and concentration, not in a logo slide.

Look at term length, renewal mechanics, termination rights, custom obligations, and how much of the number sits in the top accounts. A messy contract base is not a rounding error in a process. It is the process.

**Checklist**

- [ ] Top 10 accounts by revenue, with renewal dates and relationship owners
- [ ] Concentration view (top 5, top 10, top segment)
- [ ] Contract inventory: standard vs. custom, and who approved the custom
- [ ] Known side letters, unusual SLAs, and unpaid promises
- [ ] Churn and contraction for the last four quarters, with reasons
- [ ] A save and escalation path that is not only the founder

If three customers are the story, write that down. Hidden concentration is worse than named concentration.

### Lane 4: Operating cadence and knowledge

The company should produce decisions on a calendar, not on adrenaline.

Document the weekly, monthly, and quarterly commercial rhythm. Store the artifacts where someone else can find them: forecast snapshots, pricing exceptions, product changes that affect deals, and the "how we do this" notes that currently live in Slack.

**Checklist**

- [ ] Written operating cadence (who meets, what they decide, what they produce)
- [ ] A home for playbooks that the team actually opens
- [ ] Onboarding for a new commercial hire that does not require shadowing you for 90 days
- [ ] Incident and exception log for the last two quarters
- [ ] Clear handoff from sale to delivery to success
- [ ] A "bus-factor" note for critical systems and vendor logins (securely stored)

Knowledge that cannot be found is not knowledge. It is folklore.

### Lane 5: Diligence hygiene (without performing a deal)

You do not need a banker to keep the house in a state that can be examined.

Think in folders, not theater: corporate records, financials that tie out, customer lists that match the ledger, HR basics, IP and vendor map, and a short memo on known issues. Known issues, disclosed early, are cheaper than discovered issues.

**Checklist**

- [ ] Financials that tie to the commercial report leadership uses
- [ ] Customer and revenue reports that match (or a written reconciling item)
- [ ] Cap table and entity hygiene in order (your counsel's job, not a blog post)
- [ ] Vendor and tool map, including who owns the contracts
- [ ] Open legal, customer, or compliance items listed without spin
- [ ] A 2–3 page "how the business works" memo dated this quarter

Skip the glossy narrative until the files are boring and true.

## Founder Bottleneck: A Direct Test

Take the test once a quarter. Score yourself in private.

1. Could the weekly forecast review happen if you were offline?
2. Could a top-10 customer escalate and get a real answer without you?
3. Could a new seller quote standard packaging without a Slack thread?
4. Could finance and sales agree on last quarter's number in under an hour?
5. Could you take 15 consecutive days away without the pipeline freezing?

Five "yes" answers is rare. Three is a working company. Below that, you are the product.

The goal is not to vanish. The goal is to make your presence a choice.

## What to Do in the Next 30 Days

Do not build a data room on day one. Build transferability.

**Week 1.** Write the one-page motion. List top 10 accounts with owners and renewals. Pull discount distribution for the last two quarters.

**Week 2.** Name backups for the relationships you still hold. Put decision rights on one sheet. Clean the forecast to inspectable deals.

**Week 3.** Store pricing, stage definitions, and the commercial cadence where the team can find them. Kill one process that only works if you run it.

**Week 4.** Write the "how the business works" memo. List known issues. Book the conversation you have been avoiding: with your operator, your counsel, or an outside reviewer.
